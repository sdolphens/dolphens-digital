# Buyer Psychology Frameworks

Three lenses to map *why* someone actually buys a digital product. Use all three together — most purchases involve a stack of motivations, not a single one.

---

## Lens 1 — Jobs-to-be-Done (functional + emotional + social)

Every digital-product purchase is hired to do at least one of three jobs:

- **Functional job** — solve a discrete task. ("Track this month's freelance income across 4 clients.")
- **Emotional job** — produce a feeling. ("Stop feeling like I'm always behind on my finances.")
- **Social job** — signal something to others or to self. ("Look like the kind of person who has their money together.")

Cheap commodity products are usually hired for one job (functional). Premium-priced and high-converting products almost always do all three.

**Diagnostic:** if the only job you can articulate is functional, the product is priced and positioned as a commodity.

---

## Lens 2 — Maslow-meets-marketplace

Map the product to which layer of need it's actually serving:

| Layer | Digital-product examples | Copy register |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Safety / security | budget trackers, contract templates, tax sheets | calm, specific, no hype |
| Belonging | community-shaped products, identity templates, niche fan-art | "for [tribe]" language |
| Esteem | portfolio kits, founder templates, premium aesthetics | status-signal language |
| Self-actualization | journals, life-design systems, art tools | identity & growth language |
| Cognitive / curiosity | prompt packs, knowledge bases, swipe files | the "what's inside" tease |

Most weak listings are written one layer too low or too high relative to where the buyer actually is. A budget spreadsheet pitched as "self-actualization" feels grandiose. A journal pitched as "productivity" feels mechanical.

---

## Lens 3 — Identity, status, and the watching audience

For every digital product, name three audiences:

1. **The buyer's inner audience** — the version of themselves they want to become.
2. **The visible peer audience** — who they imagine might see them using/owning this. (Twitter followers, partner, coworkers, future employer.)
3. **The invisible-but-formative audience** — past selves, parents, ex-versions of their career. ("This is who I'd be if I'd had this five years ago.")

Identity purchases get the highest price tolerance. Functional purchases get the lowest.

**Diagnostic question:** if the buyer used this product successfully for 30 days, *who would they post about it to?* The answer reveals the social/identity job.

## The seven recurring buyer states

Most digital-product buyers, the moment they're considering checkout, are in one of these states. Map yours.

1. **The Restart** — "I'm starting over. I want clean tools for the new me."
2. **The Catch-up** — "I'm behind. I need to fix this fast."
3. **The Upgrade** — "I've outgrown my current tool. Take me up a tier."
4. **The Permission** — "I've been telling myself I'll start when I have the right setup. This is the setup."
5. **The Treat** — "I've earned a small purchase. Make me feel good about it."
6. **The Shortcut** — "I could build this myself. I won't. I want it done."
7. **The Curator** — "I collect things in this space. I want this one too."

Each state takes different copy:

| State | Lead with | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Restart | the clean slate, fresh-start framing | dwelling on what went wrong before |
| Catch-up | speed, deadline, immediate use | learning-curve language |
| Upgrade | sophistication signals, "graduated from X" | beginner-friendly framing |
| Permission | low-friction starting steps | overwhelming feature lists |
| Treat | aesthetic delight, tactile detail | utilitarian pitch |
| Shortcut | hours/days saved, decisions skipped | "learn to build your own" |
| Curator | rare/limited/series language | mass-market framing |

## Hidden motivations creators often miss

Run through these and ask: *which of these is secretly driving the purchase?*

- **Avoidance of a harder action.** They're buying the planner instead of doing the planning.
- **Procrastination disguised as preparation.** "Just one more template and I'll be ready."
- **Self-bribery.** "If I buy the tool I'll have to use it."
- **Group osmosis.** Saw three people in their community post about a similar product this week.
- **Comp shopping.** They've already bought the competitor and want to compare.
- **Future-self gift.** They're not buying for now-self; they're buying for the version of them in 6 months.
- **Avoidance of a free option.** The free version is *too much work to choose from* — paying *is* the curation.

Naming the real motivation in the description (gently, not accusingly) can be one of the highest-converting moves in the entire audit. Example: *"You don't need another budget template. You need to open the one you have on Sunday night. This is built so you'll actually open it."*

## How to populate the Buyer Psychology Map (Section 4)

For each of the six map fields, write items in the **buyer's internal voice**, not in your marketing voice.

Test: would the buyer say this sentence out loud, to a friend, in private? If no, rewrite.

- ❌ "Desires increased efficiency."
- ✅ "I want to stop opening my budget spreadsheet, getting overwhelmed, and closing it."

- ❌ "Aspires to be a more productive professional."
- ✅ "I want to be the person on my team who has the doc ready before the meeting, not after."

- ❌ "Fears falling behind peers."
- ✅ "Everyone I follow is shipping. I haven't shipped in four months."
