# Competitor Analysis Framework

How to read a competitor's listing and extract its **positioning DNA** in under five minutes, then use that to find the gap.

---

## What you're actually looking for

Not features. Not price alone. You're decoding:

- The one-line positioning the competitor is *implicitly* claiming.
- The buyer state they're addressing.
- The promise they're making (and the promise they're avoiding).
- What they're *not* saying — that's where the gap lives.

---

## The 7-question competitor read

For each competitor listing the user shares, answer:

1. **Headline / title positioning** — what category and audience does the title imply? Quote the title verbatim, then translate it.
2. **Thumbnail signal** — what kind of buyer does the thumbnail visually invite?
3. **Promise** — what specific outcome is offered? If none, mark "promise missing".
4. **Proof** — reviews count, screenshots, social proof, creator authority. Strong, weak, or absent?
5. **Price posture** — under-, fair-, or over-priced for the apparent depth? What does the price imply about the audience?
6. **Voice** — clinical, warm, indie, corporate, irreverent? In one word.
7. **Silence** — what is suspiciously *not* on the page? (No FAQ → low trust signal. No "who this is for" → vague targeting. No update policy → low commitment.)

---

## Pattern detection across 3–5 competitors

Once you've read 3–5 listings, look for what *all* of them are doing the same way. Those are **category defaults**. Category defaults are the easiest things to break to look distinctive.

Make a small table in your head:

| Trait | C1 | C2 | C3 | C4 | C5 | Category default? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Uses "all-in-one" in title | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | – | ✓ | Yes |
| Bright pastel palette | ✓ | ✓ | – | ✓ | ✓ | Yes |
| Promises productivity boost | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | Yes (cliché) |
| Addresses a specific role | – | – | – | – | – | No (gap) |
| Shows real before/after | – | – | – | – | – | No (gap) |
| Names a method | – | – | – | – | – | No (gap) |

Three "yes" defaults and three empty rows = a clear positioning gap to occupy.

---

## What makes a competitor *legitimately* strong (don't dismiss)

When critiquing competitors, distinguish between:

- **Strong positioning** — they own a clear angle and execute it well. The right move is *not to attack them head-on*. Find an adjacent niche.
- **Strong marketing on weak positioning** — they have polished assets but a generic claim. Beatable with sharper positioning.
- **Weak everything but high social proof** — they have momentum (reviews, audience) compensating for vague positioning. Beatable, but slower.

The creator's product strategy must match which kind of competitor they're facing. Tell them which.

---

## Reverse-engineering pricing

For each competitor, ask:

- What price did they choose, and what *psychological* anchor does it set?
- Are they at $9–19 (impulse)? $24–49 (considered)? $79–149 (premium)? $200+ (investment)?
- Does the price match the *depth* implied by their listing? If they're priced at $19 but the listing implies professional-grade depth, they're under-pricing — leaves room for the user to come in at $39 with a more premium feel.
- Conversely, if they're priced at $79 but the listing looks $19, they're either failing or have audience leverage you don't.

---

## The "what they'd never say" probe

For each competitor, ask: *what is true about the buyer that this competitor would never put in their listing because it would feel too honest?*

Examples:

- A productivity template competitor would never say: *"You'll probably download this and not open it for two weeks."*
- A budget spreadsheet competitor would never say: *"This won't fix your spending. It just makes you see it."*
- A prompt-pack competitor would never say: *"Most of these prompts are common. The value is the 12 we curated for [specific use case]."*

The honest thing the category won't say is often the most converting line your creator can write.

---

## Output: the gap statement

After reading the competitor set, you must be able to write one sentence:

> The category collectively does ___, ___, and ___. No one is doing ___, even though buyers obviously want it because ___.

This sentence becomes the seed of:
- Section 2 (Differentiation Analysis)
- Section 9 (Niche Gap Analysis)
- At least two of the 10 angles in Section 3

If you cannot write this sentence, you have not analyzed the competitors deeply enough.

---

## When the user hasn't given you competitors

Ask for 2–3. If they refuse or don't know any:

- Treat that as a diagnostic signal — the creator hasn't done market mapping, which often correlates with weak positioning.
- Generate the analysis based on *plausible* category patterns, but flag every claim as `[pattern-based, verify against your real competitors]`.
- Recommend, as one of the "top three moves this week", that they spend 20 minutes screenshotting the top 10 listings in their category.
