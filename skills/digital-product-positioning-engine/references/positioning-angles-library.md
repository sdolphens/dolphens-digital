# Positioning Angles Library

A catalog of angle archetypes. When generating the 10 angles in Section 3, pick from across families so the angles are *meaningfully* distinct, not 10 rewordings of the same idea.

Each archetype lists: the lever, when to use it, when to avoid it, and an example title pattern.

---

## Family 1 — Identity-led

### 1.1 The "what people like me use" angle
- *Lever:* in-group identification.
- *Use when:* there's a clear tribe (indie hackers, type-A planners, slow-living parents).
- *Avoid when:* the tribe is too vague to name in one phrase.
- *Pattern:* `The [tribe noun]'s [product]`. E.g., "The indie hacker's revenue dashboard."

### 1.2 The maker's-personal-system angle
- *Lever:* authority through specificity of the creator.
- *Use when:* the creator can credibly say "this is the exact system I used to…".
- *Avoid when:* the creator hasn't done the thing themselves.
- *Pattern:* `How I [specific result] — the [product] I actually use`.

### 1.3 The taste-signal angle
- *Lever:* aesthetic identity.
- *Use when:* the visual or brand world is the actual differentiator.
- *Avoid when:* the buyer's primary motivation is functional, not aesthetic.
- *Pattern:* a reference brand or magazine in the description, restrained typography in the title.

### 1.4 The graduation angle
- *Lever:* moving past a previous identity ("I'm not a beginner anymore").
- *Use when:* buyer has tried the entry-level version of this category and is ready for grown-up tools.
- *Pattern:* `[Product] for people who've outgrown [the obvious starter tool]`.

## Family 2 — Outcome / transformation-led

### 2.1 The specific-vivid-before-after
- *Lever:* concrete state change.
- *Use when:* the product reliably produces a result the buyer can picture.
- *Pattern:* `From [vivid bad state] to [vivid good state] in [time window]`.

### 2.2 The single-result angle
- *Lever:* clarity by subtraction.
- *Use when:* the product does many things but one matters most.
- *Avoid when:* buyers genuinely value the breadth (e.g., all-in-one OS).
- *Pattern:* `The [product] that just does [one thing] right`.

### 2.3 The "first time it actually works" angle
- *Lever:* relief from repeated failure.
- *Use when:* buyers have tried multiple alternatives and bounced off.
- *Pattern:* `[Outcome] — built so you actually finish it this time`.

### 2.4 The deadline / event-driven angle
- *Lever:* a buyer's near-term external pressure (launch, wedding, move, tax day).
- *Use when:* product solves a time-bound problem.
- *Pattern:* `[Product] for the [event] [N weeks] away`.

---

## Family 3 — Mechanism / method-led (the "unique how")

### 3.1 The named-system angle
- *Lever:* the product contains a proprietary-feeling method.
- *Use when:* there's a real method, sequence, or framework inside.
- *Avoid when:* the method is just "open it and use it".
- *Pattern:* Name the method. `The [name] method, packaged as a [product]`.

### 3.2 The contrarian-mechanism angle
- *Lever:* the method *rejects* the category default.
- *Use when:* there's a genuine, defensible "we do it differently because…".
- *Pattern:* `Stop [common approach]. Try [this approach] instead.`

### 3.3 The constraint-as-feature angle
- *Lever:* turning a limitation into the headline benefit.
- *Use when:* the product is intentionally small/simple/single-purpose.
- *Pattern:* `One page. No setup. [Outcome].`

### 3.4 The "show your work" angle
- *Lever:* transparency over polish.
- *Use when:* buyers in the niche distrust polished marketing.
- *Pattern:* describe the messy real process, then offer the cleaned-up artifact.

---

## Family 4 — Contrarian / anti-category

### 4.1 The anti-bloat angle
- *Lever:* fatigue with maximalist competitors.
- *Use when:* category is dominated by "mega-pack", "all-in-one", "1000+ prompts".
- *Pattern:* `The opposite of a mega-pack — [N] [items] you'll actually use`.

### 4.2 The "category is broken" angle
- *Lever:* indictment of the whole category, repositioned as a new category.
- *Use when:* the creator can credibly name why the category fails.
- *Risky:* requires confidence and a real argument, not just vibes.

### 4.3 The "for people the category ignores" angle
- *Lever:* serving a deliberately excluded audience.
- *Use when:* you can name who the category doesn't speak to (variable-income earners, neurodivergent users, etc.).

### 4.4 The de-aspiration angle
- *Lever:* refusing the aspirational frame.
- *Use when:* buyers are tired of being sold a fantasy.
- *Pattern:* `This won't change your life. It will [smaller, real claim].`

---

## Family 5 — Status / signal

### 5.1 The "what experts actually use" angle
- *Lever:* borrowed authority.
- *Use when:* there's a real expert/practitioner cohort that uses this kind of tool.
- *Pattern:* `[Product] modeled on what [specific role] actually does.`

### 5.2 The visible-result angle
- *Lever:* the buyer wants others to notice.
- *Use when:* the outcome is publicly visible (portfolio, social, home, desk).
- *Pattern:* lead with the visible artifact, not the internal process.

### 5.3 The "scarce taste" angle
- *Lever:* signaling discernment.
- *Use when:* visual or curatorial restraint is the differentiator.
- *Pattern:* fewer items, more weight per item, no mass-market language.

---

## Family 6 — Time-arbitrage / shortcut

### 6.1 The "skip the boring part" angle
- *Lever:* paying to avoid drudgery.
- *Pattern:* `[N hours] of [tedious task] — done, packaged, ready`.

### 6.2 The "decisions-made-for-you" angle
- *Lever:* relief from decision fatigue.
- *Use when:* the category is choice-overloaded (color palettes, font pairings, prompt-engineering).
- *Pattern:* `[N] decisions you don't have to make`.

### 6.3 The "no learning curve" angle
- *Lever:* zero-onboarding promise.
- *Pattern:* `Open it. It works. No tutorial.`

---

## Family 7 — Niche-collision

These take a product into an adjacent audience that doesn't currently know it exists.

### 7.1 The cross-domain port
- *Lever:* applying a tool from one field to another (e.g., a sales CRM template ported for freelance illustrators).
- *Use when:* the source category's tools are over-built for the target audience's needs.

### 7.2 The life-event angle
- *Lever:* attaching the product to a major life moment (move, baby, divorce, job loss, career pivot).
- *Use when:* the product genuinely helps in that moment, even if originally designed for general use.

### 7.3 The micro-niche angle
- *Lever:* shrinking the audience until the product becomes "the only one for them".
- *Pattern:* not "wedding planner" but "destination wedding planner for couples paying their own way".

---

## How to mix angles for the 10-angle output

Aim for variety. A balanced set looks like:

- 2 from Family 1 (identity)
- 2 from Family 2 (outcome)
- 2 from Family 3 (mechanism)
- 1 from Family 4 (contrarian)
- 1 from Family 5 (status)
- 1 from Family 6 (shortcut)
- 1 from Family 7 (niche-collision)

If one family is *obviously* the strongest fit for this product, you may take 3 from it, but explain that choice in a single line above the angle list.
