# Core Analysis Framework

This is the internal diagnostic engine. Run all eight passes silently, then translate findings into the output sections. Do not paste raw diagnostic notes to the user.

## Pass 1 — Generic-ness audit

Ask of the current offer:

- Could this title be swapped onto a competitor's product with no other change, and still make sense? If yes → generic.
- Does the description describe **features** or **outcomes**? Feature-heavy = generic. (Outcomes ≠ benefits-speak; outcomes are specific end-states.)
- Does the thumbnail look like 80% of the category? (Same color palette, same layout, same typography.)
- Does the listing rely on category-default keywords (e.g., "minimal", "aesthetic", "all-in-one", "easy-to-use") as differentiators?
- Could a buyer describe this product to a friend in one sentence that *isn't* its literal name?

Score generic-ness 1–5 and write a one-line verdict.

## Pass 2 — Market saturation read

Look at the competitor examples provided (and your knowledge of the category):

- Is the category **flooded with near-identical products**? (e.g., "ADHD planner template", "ChatGPT prompt mega-pack".)
- Are competitors competing on **price**, **aesthetics**, or **identity**? Price-competition signals commoditization.
- Is there a **single dominant brand** owning the category? Hard to enter, but tells you the positioning to *avoid*.
- Are there **micro-niches** within the category that no one is serving? (e.g., not "wedding planner" but "second-marriage low-key elopement planner".)

Output: saturation level (low / medium / high / commoditized) + 1–3 micro-niche openings.

## Pass 3 — Buyer sophistication level

Eugene Schwartz's awareness stages, adapted for digital products:

1. **Unaware** — doesn't know the problem exists (rare for paid digital products; usually free-content territory).
2. **Problem-aware** — knows they have a problem, doesn't know solutions exist. Sell the *named problem*.
3. **Solution-aware** — knows solutions exist, comparing approaches. Sell the *unique mechanism*.
4. **Product-aware** — knows your product exists, hasn't bought. Sell the *risk reversal / proof*.
5. **Most aware** — just needs the offer. Sell the *deal / urgency*.

Pick the dominant level. Most digital-product buyers are 3 or 4. Adjust copy register accordingly — solution-aware buyers tune out problem-agitation copy.

## Pass 4 — Emotional motivation map

Behind every digital-product purchase, name the *real* motivation. Common patterns:

- **Identity purchase** — "this product is what someone like me uses". (Notion power-users, indie hackers, design Twitter.)
- **Aspirational shortcut** — "if I own this, I'm closer to who I want to be".
- **Anxiety relief** — "this will stop me feeling behind / disorganized / unprepared".
- **Status signal** — "owning/using this is visible to my peers".
- **Permission slip** — "buying this lets me feel allowed to start".
- **Decision avoidance** — "I want someone else to have made the choices for me".
- **Curiosity / novelty** — "I want to see what's inside".
- **Time arbitrage** — "I'm paying to skip the boring 6 hours".
- **Hope of transformation** — "this will finally be the one that works".
- **Completionism** — "I collect these / I want the set".

Name the top 1–2 motivations. These drive the angles in section 3.

## Pass 5 — Transformation perceived

Map current state → desired state, with maximum specificity:

- *Before*: what does the buyer's day, desk, week, project, or feeling look like right now?
- *After*: what does it look like 1 day, 1 week, 1 month after using this?
- *Visible to others*: what would someone else notice changed about the buyer?

If you can't name a vivid before/after, the product is positioned too vaguely.

## Pass 6 — Thumbnail / visual audit

If a thumbnail or mockup was provided:

- **3-second test** — what does a buyer absorb in three seconds? Name three elements they'd see.
- **Hierarchy** — is there one clear focal point, or is everything competing?
- **Contrast** — does it stand out in a grid of category competitors, or vanish?
- **Promise legibility** — is the *what you get* readable at thumbnail size (≤300px)?
- **Identity cue** — does the visual signal who this is *for* without text?
- **Curiosity cue** — is there a partial-reveal or open loop, or is everything shown?

If no thumbnail provided, work from the description's visual implications.

## Pass 7 — Pricing & offer logic

- **Anchor**: is the price perceived in isolation, or against an obvious comp?
- **Tier structure**: single SKU, or tiers? A second tier almost always lifts conversion of the cheaper one.
- **Bundle math**: would 2–3 adjacent products bundled lift AOV?
- **Bonus stack**: does the offer include any moats (templates + community + updates + license)?
- **Format friction**: PDF vs. editable vs. live tool — does the format match buyer expectation for the price?
- **Floor and ceiling**: where's the realistic floor (race-to-bottom risk) and ceiling (premium tier potential) for this category?

Output: pricing posture (under-priced / fair / over-priced) + 2–3 offer-design moves.

## Pass 8 — Positioning gaps competitors are missing

For each provided competitor (or representative competitor patterns):

- What is their one-line positioning?
- What buyer state are they speaking to?
- What are they *not* saying that's true and valuable?
- Where do they pile up against each other (same hook, same visual, same audience)?

The gap is the space *no competitor is occupying* that *real buyers want*. That gap becomes the differentiation angle in section 2 and at least 2 of the 10 positioning angles in section 3.

## Self-test before writing

Before producing the deliverable, you must be able to complete these sentences:

- "The buyer is not actually buying a [product type]. They are buying ___."
- "This product currently looks identical to competitors because ___."
- "The single largest lever this creator can pull this week is ___."
- "The risk in following my advice is ___."

If any sentence is hand-wavy, redo the relevant pass.
