# Pricing & Offer Design

How to think about price, tiers, bundles, bonuses, and the offer structure surrounding a digital product. Use this for Section 8.

---

## First principle: price is positioning

Buyers don't evaluate price in isolation. They evaluate it against:

- The visual sophistication of the listing.
- The depth implied by the description.
- The named competitor / alternative they're mentally comparing.
- The buyer state (Restart / Catch-up / Upgrade / Treat — see buyer-psychology-frameworks.md).

A correctly priced product *confirms* the buyer's read of the listing. A mispriced product *contradicts* it — and contradiction kills conversion.

---

## Digital-product price zones (rough heuristics, not laws)

| Zone | Price | Buyer's mental frame | Required to justify |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Impulse | $5–19 | "Sure, why not." | Clear single use, no buyer's-remorse risk |
| Considered | $24–49 | "This looks useful, let me check the inside." | Specific outcome, depth proof |
| Premium | $59–149 | "This is the one I'm buying instead of three cheaper ones." | Clear superiority + sophistication signals |
| Investment | $179–499 | "This is for serious work." | Method, system, or live support |
| Studio / pro | $500+ | "This is a tool for my business." | License terms, ongoing value, identity-of-buyer fit |

Crossing a zone requires changing the visual language and the depth of the listing, not just the number.

## The two-tier rule

Single-SKU listings under-convert. Adding a second tier — even a barely-different one — almost always lifts overall conversion, because:

- The cheaper tier no longer carries the full pricing decision alone.
- The more expensive tier acts as an anchor making the cheaper one feel reasonable.
- Some buyers were always going to pay more; the single-SKU left that money on the table.

**Minimum viable two-tier:**

- **Standard** — the current product.
- **Plus** — the product + 1–2 cheap-to-add bonuses (cheat-sheet, swipe file, future-update access, commercial license).

Price gap: roughly 1.6–2.2× standard. ($29 → $49, $49 → $89, etc.)

## Bundle logic

A bundle works when:

1. The bundled items share a buyer state (all are Restart-state products, or all are Upgrade-state).
2. The bundle answers a *bigger* job-to-be-done than any single item.
3. The discount is real but small (typically 15–25%), preserving the price-anchor of each item sold separately.

A bundle fails when:

- Items don't naturally co-purchase.
- Bundle is just a discount, not a re-positioning.
- The bundle name is a feature list ("3 Templates Bundle") rather than a stated outcome ("The 30-day relaunch kit").

Always *name* the bundle around the buyer's situation, not its contents.

## Pricing anchors on the listing itself

Visible anchors that lift conversion:

- **Crossed-out higher price.** Use only if it reflects a real prior price or a real adjacent product price.
- **"Compared to [legit alternative] at $X"** — works only with credible comparisons.
- **The tier above** — the existence of a $89 tier makes the $39 tier feel safer.
- **Per-use math** — "Used twice = $0.50 per use" for templates designed to be reopened.

Avoid:

- Fake scarcity timers.
- Fake "$199 → $19!!" discounts that scream low-effort.
- Anchors that have no real referent.

## Bonus stacking that actually works

Effective bonuses share three traits: they're *cheap to deliver*, *valuable to the specific buyer*, and *believable*.

Strong bonus types for digital products:

- **A cheat-sheet / one-page summary** of the main product.
- **A decision tree / flowchart** the buyer can use without opening the main product.
- **A swipe file** of examples.
- **Future updates included** (especially for templates and prompt packs).
- **A commercial-use license upgrade.**
- **A small adjacent product** the creator already owns.
- **A community-invite** (only if a real community exists).
- **A live walkthrough recording** (one-time investment, replays forever).

Weak / suspicious bonuses:

- "Bonus: a PDF list of resources" (smells like AI-generated filler).
- Vague "exclusive access" promises.
- Bonuses worth more than the main product (breaks trust).

## Upsell paths

Where to place upsells:

1. **On the product page itself** — a "Plus" tier or a "see also" pairing.
2. **At checkout** — a one-click add-on with a small price (often 30–40% of the main price).
3. **Post-purchase email (24–72 hrs in)** — when the buyer has experienced the product, offer the deeper version.
4. **In-product** — inside the template/PDF, a quiet pointer to a more advanced product. Never aggressive.

Avoid upselling a buyer to a *fundamentally similar* product. Upsell to depth, to community, or to live help.

## When to recommend raising the price

Recommend a price increase if:

- The listing visuals and depth already match the next zone up.
- Competitors at the next price zone don't visibly out-deliver this product.
- The creator has a real, defensible audience or reputation.
- The current price is generating low conversion *and* low perceived value (a classic mispriced-low product).

Counter-intuitively, raising price often *lifts* conversion when the original price was contradicting the listing's quality signals.

---

## When to recommend lowering the price (rare)

Almost never. The usual problems blamed on "too expensive" are actually:

- Weak thumbnail (low traffic, not low conversion).
- Vague promise (browsers, not buyers).
- No proof (high perceived risk).

Lower price only if the product is genuinely thin for its zone and a richer offer can't be assembled.

## The premium tier sketch

In Section 8, sketch a "Pro / Studio / Lifetime" tier even if the creator hasn't built one. The sketch should include:

- Tier name (specific to this product's world, not a generic "Pro").
- Two or three additions the tier would credibly include (not five).
- Price (typically 2.5–4× the standard tier).
- The audience this tier targets (a *narrower*, more committed slice).
- The minimum thing the creator must build before launching this tier.

Honesty rule: if no premium tier is credible yet, say so explicitly and list what would need to exist first. Don't invent a premium tier just to fill the section.
